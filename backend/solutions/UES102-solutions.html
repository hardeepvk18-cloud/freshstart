Q2 (a)12 marksTurning — cutting velocity and MRR
A straight turning operation is performed on a lathe to reduce the diameter of a cylindrical work-piece from 25 mm to 21 mm over an effective machining length of 150 mm. The material removal is carried out in multiple passes, with a depth of cut of 1 mm in each pass. The tool operates with a constant feed rate of 0.25 mm per revolution, and the process produces 10 components per hour. Assume that the spindle speed remains constant during all passes, determine the cutting velocity employed in each pass and the corresponding material removal rate (MRR) in mm³/min.

Step 1 — how many passes

Depth of cut is measured on the radius, but the diameter drops by twice that each pass.

radial stock to remove = (25 − 21) / 2 = 2 mm depth of cut per pass = 1 mm number of passes = 2 / 1 = 2 so the diameter goes 25 → 23 → 21 mm

Step 2 — spindle speed from the production rate

10 components an hour fixes the time, and the time fixes N — this is the step the question is really testing.

time per component = 60 / 10 = 6 min time per pass = 6 / 2 = 3 min revolutions needed for one pass: L / f = 150 / 0.25 = 600 rev N = 600 rev ÷ 3 min = 200 rev/min

Step 3 — cutting velocity in each pass

N is the same in both passes, but the diameter at the cut is not — so the cutting velocity drops in the second pass. That is exactly why the question says "in each pass".

V = π D N pass 1, cutting at D = 25 mm: V₁ = π × 25 × 200 = 15 708 mm/min = 15.71 m/min pass 2, cutting at D = 23 mm: V₂ = π × 23 × 200 = 14 451 mm/min = 14.45 m/min

Step 4 — material removal rate

MRR = V · f · d, with V taken at the mean diameter of that pass, because the cut spans from the entry diameter to the exit diameter.

pass 1: mean D = (25 + 23)/2 = 24 mm V = π × 24 × 200 = 15 080 mm/min MRR₁ = 15 080 × 0.25 × 1 = 3 770 mm³/min pass 2: mean D = (23 + 21)/2 = 22 mm V = π × 22 × 200 = 13 823 mm/min MRR₂ = 13 823 × 0.25 × 1 = 3 456 mm³/min average MRR = (3 770 + 3 456)/2 = 3 613 mm³/min

Check it a second way

Work out the volume of metal actually removed and divide by the time. If this does not match the average above, something is wrong.

volume = (π/4)(25² − 21²) × 150 = (π/4)(625 − 441) × 150 = (π/4)(184)(150) = 21 677 mm³ MRR = 21 677 / 6 min = 3 613 mm³/min ✓ matches
Answers
N = 200 rev/min · V₁ = 15.71 m/min, V₂ = 14.45 m/min · MRR₁ = 3 770, MRR₂ = 3 456 mm³/min (average 3 613 mm³/min)
Where marks go

Two traps. Taking the depth of cut as 4 mm instead of 2 mm — the diameter falls by 4, the radius by 2. And giving one cutting velocity when the question asks for the velocity "in each pass": the whole point is that V falls as the diameter falls, even though N is held constant.

Q2 (b)8 marksSingle point cutting tool — signature
A single point cutting tool has the following tool signature: 6° – 8° – 2° – 2° – 12° – 15° – 0.8 mm. Identify each element in the tool signature and show them schematically in Top view, Side view and Front view of the single point cutting tool. Identify the angles that prevent rubbing in a cutting tool and discuss the consequences of rubbing during machining.

Step 1 — the seven elements, in ASA order

A tool signature is always written in the same order, and that order is what the first marks are for. In the ASA (American Standards Association) system it is: back rake, side rake, end relief, side relief, end cutting edge angle, side cutting edge angle, nose radius.

ValueElementWhat it does
6°Back rake angleTilts the face along the tool axis; steers the chip away from the machined surface
8°Side rake angleTilts the face sideways; controls chip flow direction and the cutting force
2°End relief (clearance) angleKeeps the tool's end flank off the just-cut face
2°Side relief (clearance) angleKeeps the tool's side flank off the machined surface
12°End cutting edge angleStops the trailing edge dragging on the finished surface
15°Side cutting edge angleSets how the tool enters the cut and spreads the load over a longer edge
0.8 mmNose radiusBlends the two edges; larger radius gives a better finish and a stronger nose

Step 2 — where each angle shows up in which view

A question that says "show them schematically in top, side and front view" is asking you to sort the seven angles into the three views. Each angle appears in exactly one.

TOP VIEW (looking down on the tool face) side cutting edge angle 15° end cutting edge angle 12° nose radius 0.8 mm SIDE VIEW (looking along the work axis, at the side flank) side rake angle 8° side relief angle 2° FRONT VIEW (looking at the end of the tool, at the end flank) back rake angle 6° end relief angle 2°

Draw three boxes, label the tool in each, and mark only the angles listed for that view. Put the workpiece surface in as a reference line — the relief angles mean nothing without it, because they are measured from it.

Step 3 — which angles prevent rubbing

The RELIEF (clearance) angles: end relief 2° keeps the end flank clear of the freshly cut face side relief 2° keeps the side flank clear of the machined surface Both are the 2° values in this signature. Rake angles do not prevent rubbing — they control the chip, not the flank contact.

Step 4 — what rubbing does

  • Heat. The flank scrapes the work instead of clearing it, and friction there produces heat right at the cutting edge, where the tool is weakest.
  • Flank wear. That rubbing wears a land on the flank, and once it starts it grows, which increases the contact area, which increases the rubbing — it feeds itself.
  • Poor surface finish. The rubbed surface is smeared and torn rather than cut, and it work-hardens, which makes the next pass worse.
  • Higher forces and power. The extra friction adds to the cutting force with no extra metal removed, so power goes up and the machine may chatter.
  • Dimensional error. As the flank wears, the tool effectively retreats from the work and the diameter creeps upward through the cut.

If the relief angle is made too large instead, the wedge behind the edge becomes thin and the edge chips — so there is a sensible minimum and a sensible maximum, and 2° is at the small end, typical of a tool cutting a soft material where the edge needs support.

Where marks go

Getting the signature order wrong. It is back rake first, then side rake — not the other way round — and the two 2° values are the relief angles, not the cutting edge angles. If you cannot remember the order, remember that it runs rake → relief → cutting edge angles → nose radius, and the back/end pair always comes before the side pair within each type.

Process: THREAD ROLLING (a cold forming process) How it works: the blank is fed between two hardened dies carrying the thread profile in reverse. Q1 (b)7 marksAdditive manufacturing
Write the basic steps of part manufacturing through additive manufacturing (3D printing). As an engineer, which type of Additive manufacturing process you will suggest to fabricate the implant as shown in Figure 2. Explain the working principle of that particular process with a neat diagram.

Part 1 — the steps, in order

  1. 3D model. Build the part in CAD, or capture it — for an implant, from a CT or MRI scan of the patient converted to a solid model.
  2. Convert to STL. The surfaces are approximated by a mesh of triangles. Finer triangles mean a smoother part and a bigger file.
  3. Orient and support. Choose how the part sits on the bed — this decides surface finish, strength direction and how much support is needed — and add supports where the process requires them.
  4. Slice. The model is cut into horizontal layers and each layer is turned into a tool path, with layer thickness, speed and infill set here.
  5. Build. The machine adds material layer on layer until the part is complete.
  6. Remove and clean. Take the part off the bed, break away supports, brush off loose powder or wash off resin.
  7. Post-process. Heat treatment or hot isostatic pressing for metal parts, machining of mating faces, polishing, and for an implant, sterilisation.

Part 2 — which process for the implant

Figure 2 shows a polymer implant with an open lattice structure. That lattice is the clue: it has undercuts and internal voids everywhere, so any process that needs support structures would be impossible to clean out.

Suggested process: SELECTIVE LASER SINTERING (SLS) Why this one: • the surrounding unsintered powder supports the lattice, so no support structures have to be built inside it and then removed • it runs biocompatible polymers such as PEEK and nylon 12 • it holds fine features and thin struts accurately • loose powder shakes out of the open lattice cleanly

How SLS works — for the diagram

  1. A thin, even layer of polymer powder is spread across the build platform by a roller or blade.
  2. The whole bed is held just below the melting point, so the laser only has to supply the last small amount of energy.
  3. A CO₂ laser, steered by two galvanometer mirrors, traces the cross-section of that layer and sinters the powder grains together where it passes.
  4. The platform drops by one layer thickness, the roller spreads fresh powder, and the laser draws the next cross-section, fusing it to the one below.

Q2 (b)5 marksChip welding / built-up edge
With the aid of a schematic, illustrate the phenomenon of chip welding. Under what machining conditions is it likely to occur, and what strategies can be employed to minimize or eliminate it?

What it is

Chip welding is the built-up edge, or BUE. Under the pressure and heat where the chip slides over the tool face, some of the work material seizes and pressure-welds itself to the rake face just behind the cutting edge.

The cycle, which is what the schematic should show: 1. a layer of work material sticks to the rake face 2. it work-hardens and grows, and starts doing the cutting itself 3. it becomes unstable and breaks away 4. part goes off with the chip, part is dragged onto the machined surface, leaving it rough and scored 5. a fresh layer sticks and the cycle starts again Draw: tool, chip curling away, and a wedge of built-up material sitting on the rake face ahead of the tool, with the effective cutting edge now at the tip of that wedge, not the tool.

When it happens

  • Low to medium cutting speed. This is the main one. At low speed there is time for the material to weld; above a critical speed the temperature is high enough that the material softens and flows instead of sticking, and the BUE disappears.
  • Ductile work material — low carbon steel, aluminium, copper. Brittle materials give discontinuous chips and do not form a BUE.
  • Small or negative rake angle, which raises the pressure on the chip–tool contact.
  • High feed and depth of cut, again through higher contact pressure.
  • No cutting fluid, so nothing prevents the metal-to-metal contact.

How to stop it

  • Raise the cutting speed past the critical value. Cheapest and most effective fix.
  • Increase the rake angle — a larger positive rake cuts the contact pressure and lets the chip slide away.
  • Use a cutting fluid, chosen for lubrication rather than cooling, so a film separates chip from tool.
  • Reduce the feed, which lowers the chip thickness and the pressure with it.
  • Use a tool with a low affinity for the work — a coated carbide (TiN, TiAlN) or a polished rake face, so the chip does not weld to it.
Where marks go

Saying BUE is always bad. A small stable built-up edge actually protects the tool by taking the wear itself, and in rough cutting it can extend tool life. What ruins the job is the unstable BUE that keeps breaking off and smearing the surface — say that, and the answer reads like you understand it rather than remember it.

Q3 (c)5 marksForging — flash
The given Figure 4 shows some unwanted material. Identify it, explain why it is produced. Suggest and illustrate schematically the suitable forging process in which this unwanted material is not formed.

What it is

The unwanted material is FLASH — the thin fin of metal squeezed out around the parting line of the connecting rod in Figure 4.

Why it is produced — and why it is deliberate

This is the part most answers get wrong. Flash is not an accident or a sign of a badly made die. It is designed in, and it does a job.

  1. The billet is always given slightly more volume than the cavity needs, because you cannot cut billets to an exact volume in production.
  2. As the dies close, the excess has to go somewhere, and it escapes through a deliberate narrow gap at the parting line called the flash land.
  3. That gap is thin and long, so the metal escaping through it cools fast and meets high friction. Both make it hard to push.
  4. The resistance builds up back-pressure inside the cavity, and it is that back-pressure that forces the metal into the thin ribs and sharp corners of the part. Without flash, the far corners would not fill.

So flash is the price paid for a completely filled cavity. It is trimmed off afterwards in a trimming press, and it is scrap — typically 10 to 20 % of the billet.

The process that avoids it

FLASHLESS FORGING (also called true closed-die or precision forging) The cavity is fully enclosed — the dies seal against each other and there is no gap at the parting line, so no metal can escape. What that demands: • the billet volume must be controlled accurately, because any excess has nowhere to go and will over-load the dies • too little material and the part will not fill • higher die stresses, so stronger and more expensive tooling • usually done on a press with good stroke control, not a hammer What you get: • no flash, so no trimming operation and no trimming scrap • much better material utilisation • closer tolerances and less machining afterwards

For the sketch, draw them side by side: impression die forging with the thin flash escaping at the parting line, and flashless forging with the cavity fully sealed and the billet exactly filling it. One arrow each showing where the metal goes.

Answer
The unwanted material is flash, formed deliberately at the flash land to build back-pressure and fill the cavity · avoided by flashless (true closed die) forging, which needs accurate billet volume
Where marks go

Saying flash is simply "extra material that leaks out". That gets one mark. Explaining that the flash land builds back-pressure, and that the back-pressure is what fills the thin sections, gets the rest — and it is the sentence that shows you know why anyone would design flash on purpose.

Q2 (c)6 marksAdditive manufacturing

The words "PLA wire as feedstock" settle it. Wire on a spool means an extrusion process, and for PLA that is FDM. If the feedstock had been powder it would be SLS; if resin, SLA.

Process: FUSED DEPOSITION MODELLING (FDM), also called Fused Filament Fabrication How it works, for the schematic: 1. Q2 (b)4 marksForging — hand tools
What is the key process involved in the manufacturing of hand tools such as spanners and wrenches, and provide a schematic explanation of this process?
Process: CLOSED (IMPRESSION) DIE DROP FORGING, hot.

Why it is the right process

  • A spanner is a high-stress, high-volume part — it is loaded hard at the jaws every time it is used, and it is made in millions. Both point at impression die forging.
  • Grain flow. Forging bends the grain to follow the outline of the jaws and the shank. A spanner machined from plate would have its fibres cut through right at the jaw fillet, which is where it would break.
  • Material saving. The shape is mostly thin shank with two bulky ends — machining it from bar would waste most of the bar.
  • Near net shape, so only the jaw faces need finishing.

The sequence — for the schematic

  1. Cut and heat the bar stock to forging temperature.
  2. Fullering and edging — the metal is distributed along the length, thinned where the shank will be and gathered where the two heads will be.
  3. Blocking — a rough impression brings it close to the final outline.
  4. Finishing impression — the final blow fills the cavity completely, with the excess escaping as flash at the parting line.
  5. Trimming — the flash is sheared off in a trimming die.
  6. Heat treatment, then machining and broaching of the jaw opening, then finishing and plating.

Draw: the two die halves with the spanner cavity, the hot billet between them, and flash squeezing out at the parting plane. Beside it, the progression of shapes from bar → fullered → blocked → finished, and an outline of the spanner with the grain flow lines drawn following its contour round the jaws.

Where marks go

The grain flow sketch. Anyone can write "drop forging"; the marks are in showing the fibres curving round the jaw instead of running straight through it, and saying that is why the forged spanner does not crack at the fillet.

Q4 (b)3 marksTypes of chips
What are the different types of chips formed during machining of pure copper (Cu) and Cast Iron (C.I.)? Explain the machining condition that promotes the chip formation for each material schematically.

Pure copper — continuous chip, with a built-up edge

Copper is very ductile and very soft, so the material ahead of the tool flows plastically up the rake face in one unbroken ribbon instead of fracturing. Conditions that produce it: • ductile work material (copper, aluminium, mild steel) • large POSITIVE rake angle • high cutting speed • small feed and small depth of cut • good lubrication, and a sharp, polished tool The complication: copper is so soft and so prone to sticking that at low and medium speed it forms a BUILT-UP EDGE — material pressure-welds to the rake face, grows, breaks off, and smears the machined surface. So copper usually gives a "continuous chip with built-up edge" unless the speed is taken high enough to suppress it.

Cast iron — discontinuous chip

Cast iron is brittle, and its graphite flakes act as ready-made crack starters. The material ahead of the tool cracks rather than flows, so the chip comes away in small separate pieces. Conditions that produce it: • brittle work material (cast iron, bronze, brass) • small or NEGATIVE rake angle • low cutting speed • large feed and large depth of cut • no cutting fluid needed for chip control Side effects worth one line: the cut is intermittent, so the force fluctuates and the machine can chatter — which is why cast iron is cut on rigid machines with strong, negative-rake carbide tools.

The schematic

Two sketches side by side, each with the workpiece, the tool and the shear plane. For copper, draw one long ribbon curling away continuously from the rake face; add a small wedge on the rake face if you are showing the built-up edge version. For cast iron, draw several short separate segments breaking off at the shear plane and falling away.

Answer
Copper — continuous chip (with built-up edge at low and medium speed): ductile material, positive rake, high speed, small feed · Cast iron — discontinuous chip: brittle material, negative rake, low speed, large feed
Where marks go

Answering just "continuous and discontinuous". The question says "explain the machining condition that promotes" each, so each chip type needs its list of conditions — rake, speed, feed, and the material property behind it. Three marks, two chips, and the conditions are where the third mark lives.
