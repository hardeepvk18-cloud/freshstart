Q16 marksThevenin's theorem
Find the Thevenin's equivalent circuit for the network in Fig. 1 from the load terminals a and b. R1 = 5 Ω sits in parallel with a 10 A source; that pair is in series with R2 = 4 Ω to terminal b.

Step 1 — read the topology before touching a formula

The 5 Ω and the 10 A source share both of their ends, so they are in parallel, and that pair sits between the left node and terminal a. R2 = 4 Ω runs from the left node down to b. Getting this one fact right is most of the question: if you read the 5 Ω as being in series with the source instead, every number afterwards is wrong.

Step 2 — open-circuit voltage

With a–b open no current can leave at a. The 10 A the source pushes has nowhere to go except back through the 5 Ω, so the 5 Ω carries the full 10 A and the pair delivers zero net current into the left node. No current in R2 means no drop across it, so the left node sits at 0 V.

current in the 5 ohm = 10 A (it simply circulates with the source) V(left node) = 0 V (R2 carries nothing, so no drop) V_th = V_a - V_b = 10 A x 5 ohm = 50 V, with a positive

Step 3 — Thevenin resistance

Turn the independent current source off, which means open it (a dead current source is an open circuit, not a short — this is the single most common slip in this question). What is left from a to b is 5 Ω then 4 Ω in series.

R_th = 5 + 4 = 9 ohm

Step 4 — check it a second, independent way

Short a to b and find the short-circuit current; Rth must come out as Vth/Isc. This catches a wrong topology, which the first method alone would not.

with a-b shorted: V(left node) = -200/9 V I_sc = 10 + (-200/9)/5 = 50/9 = 5.556 A R_th = V_th / I_sc = 50 / 5.556 = 9 ohm - agrees
Answer
Vth = 50 V (terminal a positive), Rth = 9 Ω. The equivalent is a 50 V source in series with 9 Ω.
Where marks go

2 marks for Vth, 2 for Rth, 2 for the drawn equivalent circuit with polarity marked. Opening the current source rather than shorting it is where most of the lost marks are.

Q26 marksNodal analysis
Apply nodal analysis to determine the voltage VL for the network in Fig. 2. Three branches (R1 = 5 Ω with E1 = 10 V, R2 = 4 Ω with E2 = 16 V, R3 = 2 Ω with E3 = 8 V) and RL = 3 Ω all hang between one top node and the bottom rail.

Step 1 — one node does the whole job

Every element in this circuit runs from the same top rail to the same bottom rail, so there is exactly one unknown node voltage. Call it V; that is also VL, because RL is connected straight across the same two rails.

Step 2 — the polarity of E2 is the trap

E1 and E3 are drawn with + at the top of the source, so the bottom of R1 sits at +10 V and the bottom of R3 at +8 V. E2 is drawn the other way round — − at the top, + at the bottom — so the bottom of R2 sits at −16 V, not +16 V. Miss this and you get 7.79 V instead of 1.56 V.

branch bottoms, measured from the bottom rail: through E1: +10 V (+ at top) through E2: -16 V (- at top) <-- the reversed one through E3: +8 V (+ at top)

Step 3 — write KCL and solve

Take every branch current as leaving the top node and set the sum to zero.

(V - 10)/5 + (V + 16)/4 + (V - 8)/2 + V/3 = 0 multiply through by 60: 12(V - 10) + 15(V + 16) + 30(V - 8) + 20V = 0 12V - 120 + 15V + 240 + 30V - 240 + 20V = 0 77V - 120 = 0 V = 120/77 = 1.558 V

Step 4 — check by adding the branch currents

The four currents must sum to zero. They do, to machine precision — and notice how large they are compared with the answer: the branches are fighting each other, which is exactly why VL ends up small.

R1 : -1.68831 A R2 : +4.38961 A R3 : -3.22078 A RL : +0.51948 A --------- sum 0.00000 A
Answer
VL = 120/77 = 1.558 V, and IL = VL/3 = 40/77 = 0.519 A downward.
Where marks go

1 mark for identifying the single node, 3 for the node equation with the correct source polarities, 2 for solving and stating VL. An ugly fraction is not a sign of error here — 1/5 + 1/4 + 1/2 + 1/3 = 77/60, so the 77 is unavoidable.

Q36 marksSource conversion
Apply the source conversion method to compute the current passing through RL for the network in Fig. 3. I1 = 10 mA with R1 = 2 kΩ, I2 = 8 mA with R2, RL = 6.8 kΩ, I3 = 4 mA with R3 = 8.2 kΩ.

A reading of the scan that has to be flagged

On the scan R2 reads 4.7 Ω, but every other resistor in the figure is in the kilohm range and they are all standard E24 values — 2 k, 6.8 k, 8.2 k. A 4.7 Ω sitting among them makes no sense as a design, so this is almost certainly 4.7 kΩ with the 'k' lost in the scan. Both readings are solved below; check yours against the original paper.

Step 1 — convert each current source to a voltage source

A current source I in parallel with R becomes a voltage source IR in series with the same R. That is the whole method: it turns a circuit full of parallel sources into one loop you can walk round.

I1 || R1 : 10 mA x 2 kohm = 20 V in series with 2 kohm I2 || R2 : 8 mA x 4.7 kohm = 37.6 V in series with 4.7 kohm I3 || R3 : 4 mA x 8.2 kohm = 32.8 V in series with 8.2 kohm

Step 2 — solve (nodal used here as the cross-check)

Taking the bottom rail as reference and solving the three node voltages gives the current in RL directly. The source-conversion route and the nodal route must agree, and they do.

with R2 = 4.7 kohm: V_A = 24.645 V V_B = -2.039 V V_C = 13.755 V I through RL = 2.323 mA, flowing from C towards B with R2 = 4.7 ohm (the literal scan reading): I through RL = 0.755 mA
Answer
IRL = 2.32 mA on the 4.7 kΩ reading (0.75 mA if R2 really is 4.7 Ω). State which reading you used — an examiner will accept either if the working is right.
Where marks go

3 marks for carrying out the conversions correctly, 3 for the final current. The direction matters: the current flows from the right-hand node back towards RL, not the way the figure's arrow suggests.

Q46 marksDependent source
Find Vo in the circuit in Fig. 4. A 10 Ω vertical and a 10 Ω horizontal are fed by a 25 A source and a dependent source 2Vo, where Vo is the voltage across the horizontal 10 Ω.

Step 1 — why this one cannot be done by inspection

The 2Vo source depends on the very quantity you are asked for, so there is no order in which you can just "work it out". You have to write the circuit down as equations and let Vo appear on both sides.

Step 2 — spot the series path

The two 10 Ω resistors are in series from the right-hand node down to ground, so everything both sources inject has to come back through that same 20 Ω. Call that current I.

V2 = 20 I (both 10 ohm in series) V1 = 10 I (only the vertical one) Vo = V1 - V2 = 10I - 20I = -10 I

Step 3 — close the loop on itself

The current the two sources inject is 25 + 2Vo, and that must equal I. Substituting Vo = −10I turns it into one equation in one unknown.

I = 25 + 2 Vo = 25 + 2(-10 I) = 25 - 20 I 21 I = 25 I = 25/21 = 1.1905 A Vo = -10 I = -250/21 = -11.905 V

Step 4 — check

Put the answer back into the dependent source and see that the injected current really is I.

25 + 2(-11.905) = 1.1905 A = I - consistent nodal solution independently: V1 = 250/21, V2 = 500/21, Vo = -250/21 - same
Answer
Vo = −250/21 = −11.90 V. The minus sign is real: the right-hand node is at a higher potential than the left, so the polarity marked on the figure is the opposite of the actual one.
Where marks go

2 marks for setting up with the dependent source kept symbolic, 3 for solving, 1 for stating the sign and what it means. Writing Vo as a number too early is the usual way this one is lost.

Q56 marksRL transient
Find i(t) in the circuit of Fig. 5 for t > 0. The switch has been closed for a long time. 10 V source, 2 Ω and 3 Ω in series, inductor 1/3 H; the switch is connected across the 3 Ω.

Step 1 — what the switch actually does

The switch bridges the two ends of the 3 Ω, so while it is closed the 3 Ω is short-circuited and carries no current. It has been closed a long time, so at t = 0 it opens and the 3 Ω comes back into the circuit.

Step 2 — the initial current

Long time closed means steady state, and in steady DC an inductor behaves as a plain wire. So the current is set by the 2 Ω alone. Inductor current cannot jump, so this value carries across t = 0.

i(0-) = 10 / 2 = 5 A and i(0+) = i(0-) = 5 A

Step 3 — the final current and the time constant

With the switch open the loop resistance is 2 + 3 = 5 Ω. The time constant uses the resistance the inductor sees after the switching, never the one before.

i(inf) = 10 / 5 = 2 A tau = L/R = (1/3)/5 = 1/15 s -> 1/tau = 15

Step 4 — assemble and verify

Any first-order circuit follows x(t) = x(∞) + [x(0) − x(∞)]e−t/τ. Solving the differential equation directly gives exactly the same expression, with a difference of zero.

i(t) = 2 + (5 - 2) e^(-15t) = 2 + 3 e^(-15t) A solving (1/3) di/dt + 5 i = 10 with i(0) = 5 gives i(t) = 2 + 3 exp(-15t) difference from the formula: exactly 0 t = 0 5.000 A t = 0.05 3.417 A t = 0.2 2.149 A t -> inf 2.000 A
Answer
i(t) = 2 + 3e−15t A for t > 0, starting at 5 A and settling to 2 A with a time constant of 1/15 s (about 67 ms).
Where marks go

2 marks for i(0), 2 for i(∞) and τ, 2 for the assembled expression. Using the pre-switching resistance in τ is the standard error and costs both of the middle marks.

Q66 marksMagnetic circuit
Calculate the magnetomotive force (mmf) required to produce a flux of 0.015 Wb across an air gap 2.5 mm long, having an effective area of 200 cm².

Step 1 — convert the area before anything else

200 cm² is not 2 m². Area scales with the square of the length unit, so 1 cm² = 10−4 m². This single conversion is where most of the marks are lost on air-gap questions.

A = 200 cm^2 = 200 x 1e-4 = 0.02 m^2 l = 2.5 mm = 2.5e-3 m

Step 2 — flux density, then field strength

In air there is no iron to help, so μ = μ0 and H is simply B/μ0.

B = phi/A = 0.015 / 0.02 = 0.75 T H = B/mu0 = 0.75 / (4 pi x 1e-7) = 596 831 A/m

Step 3 — mmf

mmf is H multiplied by the length of the path it acts over — here only the gap.

mmf = H l = 596 831 x 2.5e-3 = 1492 ampere-turns
Answer
mmf ≈ 1492 AT (ampere-turns).
Where marks go

2 marks for B, 2 for H, 2 for the mmf. Note how large H is in air compared with iron — a few millimetres of gap needs more mmf than a metre of iron, which is the physical point the question is testing.

Q76 marksCapacitor on AC
A capacitor of 8.0 µF takes a current of 1.0 A when the alternating voltage applied across it is 230 V. Calculate (a) the frequency of the applied voltage, and (b) the resistance to be connected in series with the capacitor to reduce the current in the circuit to 0.5 A at the same frequency.

Part (a) — find the reactance first

A pure capacitor has only reactance, so Ohm's law gives XC straight away, and the frequency follows from it.

Xc = V/I = 230 / 1.0 = 230 ohm Xc = 1/(2 pi f C) -> f = 1/(2 pi Xc C) f = 1 / (2 pi x 230 x 8e-6) = 86.50 Hz

Part (b) — R and X add as a right triangle, never straight

Adding R does not reduce the current by R's share — R and XC combine as the two sides of a right angle. XC is unchanged because the frequency is unchanged.

for I = 0.5 A: Z = V/I = 230 / 0.5 = 460 ohm Z^2 = R^2 + Xc^2 R = sqrt(460^2 - 230^2) = sqrt(158700) = 398.4 ohm

Worth noticing

Halving the current needed a resistance of 398 Ω — far more than the 230 Ω of reactance already there. That is the whole point of phasor addition: quantities at right angles do not add the way scalars do.

Answer
(a) f = 86.5 Hz. (b) R = 398.4 Ω.
Where marks go

3 marks for the frequency, 3 for the resistance. Writing R = 460 − 230 = 230 Ω is the classic wrong answer and earns nothing.

Q86 marksSeries resonance
A circuit having a resistance of 4.0 Ω, an inductance of 0.50 H and a variable capacitance in series is connected across a 100 V, 50 Hz supply. Calculate (a) the capacitance to give resonance, and (b) the voltages across the inductance and capacitance.

Part (a) — resonance means the two reactances cancel

At resonance XL = XC, so the capacitance follows from the inductance and the frequency alone — R plays no part in it.

XL = 2 pi f L = 2 pi x 50 x 0.5 = 157.08 ohm at resonance Xc = XL, so C = 1/(w^2 L) = 1/((2 pi x 50)^2 x 0.5) = 20.26 uF

Part (b) — the current is limited by R alone

With the reactances cancelled the impedance collapses to just R, so the current is as large as it can be.

I = V/R = 100 / 4 = 25 A VL = I XL = 25 x 157.08 = 3927 V VC = I XC = 25 x 157.08 = 3927 V

This answer is not a mistake — read this before crossing it out

3927 V across a component on a 100 V supply looks impossible, and every year students assume they have gone wrong here. They have not. This is voltage magnification: at resonance VL and VC are equal and exactly opposite, so they cancel each other across the pair, and only the 100 V across R survives. The magnification factor is the Q of the circuit.

Q = XL/R = 157.08/4 = 39.3 VL = VC = Q x V = 39.3 x 100 = 3927 V - consistent and VL + VC = 0 as phasors, leaving V_R = 100 V = the supply
Answer
(a) C = 20.26 µF. (b) VL = VC = 3927 V, in antiphase, so they cancel and the supply still sees only 100 V.
Where marks go

2 marks for C, 2 for the current, 2 for the two voltages. A line explaining the magnification is usually worth a mark on its own and shows the examiner you know the answer is not an arithmetic slip.

Q96 marksThree-phase star — derivation
Derive the relationship between line voltage and phase voltage, and between line current and phase current, in a 3-phase star-connected system.

Step 1 — set up the three phase voltages

In a balanced star the three phase voltages are equal in size and 120° apart, measured from each line to the star point N.

V_RN = Vph /_ 0 V_YN = Vph /_ -120 V_BN = Vph /_ -240 (= /_ +120)

Step 2 — a line voltage is the difference of two phase voltages

VRY is measured between two lines, so it is VRN − VYN. Subtracting two equal phasors 120° apart gives one that is √3 times as long and leads by 30°.

V_RY = V_RN - V_YN = Vph /_0 - Vph /_-120 = Vph [ (1 + j0) - (-0.5 - j0.866) ] = Vph (1.5 + j0.866) = Vph x 1.732 /_ 30 so V_L = sqrt(3) x V_ph, leading the phase voltage by 30 degrees

Step 3 — the current relation is the easy half

In star, each line is in series with one phase winding — there is no junction between them for current to divide at. So whatever flows in the phase flows in the line.

I_L = I_ph
Answer
For a star connection: VL = √3 Vph (line voltage leading by 30°) and IL = Iph.
Where marks go

3 marks for the voltage derivation with the phasor subtraction actually shown, 1 for the 30° phase relation, 2 for the current relation with a reason. Quoting √3 without the subtraction gets about half.

Q106 marksTransformer — EMF equation and phasor diagram
(a) Derive the EMF equation of a transformer. (b) Draw and explain a neatly labelled phasor diagram of a transformer on load.

Part (a) — start from Faraday, not from the formula

The flux in the core is sinusoidal, φ = φm sin ωt. Faraday's law gives the induced emf in one turn, and N turns multiply it.

phi = phi_m sin(wt) e = -N dphi/dt = -N w phi_m cos(wt) peak emf = N w phi_m = N (2 pi f) phi_m rms = peak / sqrt(2): E = 2 pi f N phi_m / sqrt(2) = 4.44 f N phi_m so E1 = 4.44 f N1 phi_m E2 = 4.44 f N2 phi_m and E1/E2 = N1/N2 - the turns ratio falls straight out

Part (b) — what the on-load phasor diagram has to show

Build it in this order and label every phasor; an unlabelled diagram earns very little.

1. phi_m - the core flux, drawn along the reference 2. E1 and E2 - both lag phi_m by 90 degrees (from -N dphi/dt) 3. I0 - the no-load current, leading phi_m slightly; its two parts: Iw in phase with V1 (core loss), and Imag in phase with phi_m (magnetising) 4. I2 - the secondary load current, lagging E2 by the load angle phi2 5. I2' - the primary counterpart of I2, equal to I2 x (N2/N1), drawn opposite to I2 6. I1 = I0 + I2' - phasor sum, this is the primary current 7. V1 = -E1 + I1 R1 + j I1 X1 - add the primary drops 8. V2 = E2 - I2 R2 - j I2 X2 - subtract the secondary drops

The one idea being tested

Extra load current in the secondary is immediately answered by extra current in the primary, in the exact ratio of the turns, so the core flux stays practically constant from no load to full load. Say that sentence explicitly — it is usually worth a mark.

Answer
E = 4.44 f N φm for each winding, giving E1/E2 = N1/N2; and the on-load phasor diagram built in the eight steps above.
Where marks go

3 marks for the derivation (the √2 step must be visible), 3 for a labelled phasor diagram. Leaving out the primary and secondary impedance drops turns it into a no-load diagram and loses a mark.

Q1 (a)5 marksRL growth — derivation
Derive the expression for current when a resistance R and an inductance L are connected in series and a DC supply of voltage E is applied. The circuit is initially relaxed. Draw the graph of i against t, and find i when t = the time constant.

Step 1 — write KVL and solve it as a differential equation

The inductor opposes change, so the current cannot appear instantly; that resistance to change is what the exponential describes.

E = i R + L di/dt L di/dt = E - iR di/(E - iR) = dt/L integrating: -(1/R) ln(E - iR) = t/L + K at t = 0 the circuit is relaxed, so i = 0: K = -(1/R) ln E -(1/R) ln[(E - iR)/E] = t/L (E - iR)/E = e^(-Rt/L) i = (E/R) [1 - e^(-Rt/L)]

Step 2 — the graph

It starts at zero with an initial slope of E/L, bends over, and approaches E/R without ever quite reaching it. Mark E/R as a dashed asymptote and mark the time constant on the t axis — both are expected in the sketch.

Step 3 — the value at one time constant

τ = L/R. Substituting t = τ makes the exponent exactly −1, which is where the familiar 63.2% comes from.

at t = tau = L/R: i = (E/R)[1 - e^-1] = (E/R)(1 - 0.3679) = 0.632 E/R so the current reaches 63.2% of its final value in one time constant
Answer
i = (E/R)[1 − e−Rt/L], and at t = τ the current is 0.632 E/R.
Where marks go

3 marks for the derivation with the integration shown, 1 for a graph with the asymptote and τ marked, 1 for the 63.2% figure. Quoting the result without integrating loses the three.

Q1 (b)5 marksRL transient — numerical
Find the steady state current in the circuit shown in Figure 1 when switch S is open. Also calculate the current at t = 0.6 s after the switch is closed. (20 V source, 15 Ω and 5 Ω in series, 4 H; S is connected across the 5 Ω.)

I = 20 / (15 + 5) = 1.0 A

Q2 (a)5 marksMaximum power transfer — derivation
Derive the condition for maximum power transfer through RL in a circuit having the series elements RTh (equivalent Thevenin resistance) and RL (variable load resistance) supplied by a voltage source VTh. Also find the expression for maximum power.

Step 1 — write the power in the load as a function of RL

I = V_th / (R_th + R_L) P = I^2 R_L = V_th^2 R_L / (R_th + R_L)^2

Step 2 — differentiate and set to zero

RL is the variable; Vth and Rth are fixed by the source network.

dP/dR_L = V_th^2 [ (R_th + R_L)^2 - R_L . 2(R_th + R_L) ] / (R_th + R_L)^4 set the numerator to zero: (R_th + R_L)^2 = 2 R_L (R_th + R_L) R_th + R_L = 2 R_L R_L = R_th

Step 3 — the maximum power itself

Put RL = Rth back in.

P_max = V_th^2 R_th / (2 R_th)^2 = V_th^2 / (4 R_th)

The point that is usually missed

At this condition the efficiency is only 50% — as much power is burnt in Rth as reaches the load. That is why power systems are never run at maximum power transfer; it is a communications and instrumentation criterion, not a power one.

Answer
Maximum power when RL = Rth, and then Pmax = Vth²/(4Rth), at 50% efficiency.
Where marks go

3 marks for the differentiation, 1 for Pmax, 1 for the efficiency remark.

Q3 (a)4 marksCircuit parameters from v and i
A series circuit consisting of two elements is connected to a supply voltage v = 220√2 sin(314t + 15°) V. The current in the circuit is i = 22√2 cos(314t − 30°) A. Determine the circuit's parameters and draw the phasor diagram.

Step 1 — put both waveforms in the same form first

The voltage is a sine and the current is a cosine. Comparing their angles directly is the trap in this question — convert the cosine to a sine before doing anything else.

cos(x) = sin(x + 90) i = 22 sqrt2 cos(314t - 30) = 22 sqrt2 sin(314t - 30 + 90) = 22 sqrt2 sin(314t + 60) so: V = 220 V rms at +15 deg I = 22 A rms at +60 deg

Step 2 — the phase angle tells you which two elements they are

The current angle is larger than the voltage angle, so the current leads. A leading current in a two-element series circuit means resistance and capacitance.

phi = theta_v - theta_i = 15 - 60 = -45 deg current leads voltage by 45 deg -> R and C in series

Step 3 — extract R and C

v(t) = 15 e^(-t/0.2) = 15 e^(-5t) V t = 0 15.00 V t = 0.2 s 5.52 V (one time constant, 36.8%) t = 1.0 s 0.10 V
Answer
v(0) = 15 V and v(t) = 15e−5t V for t ≥ 0.
Where marks go

2 marks for v(0), 1 for spotting that the 3 Ω leaves the circuit, 1 for τ, 1 for the expression.

Q55 marksSeries RLC on AC
A resistance of 10 Ω, an inductance of 0.16 H and a capacitance of 100 µF are connected in series across a 110 V, 50 Hz supply. Calculate (i) the phase difference between current and supply voltage, (ii) power consumed, (iii) draw the phasor diagram of supply voltage and line current.

Step 1 — the two reactances

XL = 2 pi f L = 2 pi x 50 x 0.16 = 50.27 ohm XC = 1/(2 pi f C) = 1/(2 pi x 50 x 100e-6) = 31.83 ohm X = XL - XC = 18.43 ohm (inductive, since XL > XC)

Step 2 — impedance, current, angle

Z = sqrt(R^2 + X^2) = sqrt(10^2 + 18.43^2) = 20.97 ohm I = V/Z = 110/20.97 = 5.245 A phi = arctan(X/R) = arctan(18.43/10) = 61.52 deg, lagging

Step 3 — power, checked two ways

P = V I cos(phi) = 110 x 5.245 x cos(61.52) = 275.1 W check: P = I^2 R = 5.245^2 x 10 = 275.1 W - agrees

The phasor diagram

Take I along the reference. VR = IR is in phase with I; VL = IXL leads I by 90°; VC = IXC lags I by 90°. VL and VC are directly opposed, so draw their difference, then add it to VR at right angles to get V, which ends up 61.5° ahead of I.

Answer
(i) 61.5°, current lagging. (ii) P = 275.1 W. (iii) phasor diagram as described.

2 marks for the reactances and Z, 1 for the angle, 1 for the power, 1 for the diagram. The two independent routes to P are worth showing — they cost one line and catch a wrong power factor.

Q65 marksTwo-wattmeter method
The two-wattmeter method produces wattmeter readings P1 = 1580 W and P2 = 2150 W when connected to a delta-connected load. If the line voltage is 230 V, calculate (i) the per-phase average power, (ii) the per-phase reactive power, (iii) the power factor, (iv) the per-phase impedance.

Step 1 — total power and the angle

The two readings add to the total power; their difference carries the reactive information.

P = P1 + P2 = 1580 + 2150 = 3730 W per phase = 3730/3 = 1243.3 W tan(phi) = sqrt3 (P2 - P1)/(P1 + P2) = sqrt3 (2150 - 1580)/3730 = 0.2647 phi = 14.83 deg power factor = cos(phi) = 0.9667

Step 2 — reactive power

Q = sqrt3 (P2 - P1) = sqrt3 x 570 = 987.3 VAR per phase = 987.3/3 = 329.1 VAR

Step 3 — impedance per phase

In delta the phase voltage equals the line voltage, and the phase current is the line current divided by √3.

S = sqrt(P^2 + Q^2) = 3858.5 VA IL = S/(sqrt3 VL) = 3858.5/(sqrt3 x 230) = 9.686 A Iph = IL/sqrt3 = 5.592 A Vph = VL = 230 V (delta) Zph = 230/5.592 = 41.13 ohm

Step 4 — check

3 Iph^2 R = 3 x 5.592^2 x (41.13 x 0.9667) = 3730 W which is exactly P1 + P2 - consistent
Answer
(i) 1243.3 W per phase, (ii) 329.1 VAR per phase, (iii) pf = 0.967 lagging, (iv) Zph = 41.13 Ω.
Where marks go

1 mark for the total, 2 for tanφ and the power factor, 1 for Q, 1 for the impedance. Using Vph = VL/√3 here is wrong — that is the star relation, and this load is delta.

Q75 marksIron ring with an air gap
An iron ring has an air-gap of 0.5 mm, 500 turns and a length of 150 cm. Determine the flux density when a current of 2 A flows through the coil. Assume the permeability of iron to be 300 and µ0 = 4π × 10−7 H/m.

A note on the wording

The paper writes "permeability of iron to be 300 H/m". 300 H/m is not a physical value for any material — μ0 alone is 1.26 × 10−6 H/m. It has to mean relative permeability μr = 300, and that is how it is solved here. Write that assumption down in the exam; it is the reading every solution uses.

Step 1 — the mmf is shared between iron and air

The same flux passes through both, so the mmf divides between them in proportion to their reluctances. Half a millimetre of air takes more mmf than a metre and a half of iron, which is the physical point of the question.

mmf = N I = 500 x 2 = 1000 AT mmf = H_iron x l_iron + H_gap x l_gap = (B/(mu0 mu_r)) l_iron + (B/mu0) l_gap

Step 2 — solve for B

l_iron = 1.50 - 0.0005 = 1.4995 m l_gap = 0.0005 m 1000 = (B/mu0) [ 1.4995/300 + 0.0005 ] = (B/mu0) [ 0.004998 + 0.0005 ] = (B/mu0) x 0.005498 B = 1000 x 4 pi x 1e-7 / 0.005498 = 0.2286 T

Step 3 — check the split

mmf across the iron = 0.2286/(mu0 x 300) x 1.4995 = 909 AT mmf across the gap = 0.2286/mu0 x 0.0005 = 91 AT total = 1000 AT - correct
Answer
B ≈ 0.229 T.
Where marks go

1 mark for stating the μr reading, 2 for the mmf split between iron and gap, 2 for B. Ignoring the air gap gives 0.377 T and earns almost nothing — the gap is the whole question.

Q85 marksTransformer — principle and numerical
(i) Discuss the principle of operation of a single phase transformer with a neat and clean diagram. (ii) A 1000 kVA transformer has primary and secondary turns of 400 and 100 respectively and the induced voltage in the secondary is 1000 V. Find (i) the primary voltage and (ii) the primary and secondary full load currents.

Part (i) — the principle in four sentences

Say these four things and draw the two windings on a laminated core:

1. An alternating voltage on the primary drives an alternating current, which sets up an alternating flux in the laminated iron core. 2. Almost all of that flux links the secondary winding as well. 3. By Faraday's law the changing flux induces an emf in both windings, E = 4.44 f N phi_m, so the emfs are in the ratio of the turns. 4. Nothing rotates and nothing touches: energy crosses from one winding to the other entirely through the magnetic field. This is why a transformer only works on AC - a steady DC flux induces nothing.

Part (ii) — primary voltage

E1 = E2 x N1/N2 = 1000 x 400/100 = 4000 V

Part (ii) — full load currents

The kVA rating is common to both windings.

I1 = S/E1 = 1 000 000/4000 = 250 A I2 = S/E2 = 1 000 000/1000 = 1000 A check: I1 E1 = I2 E2 = 1000 kVA - as it must be check: I1/I2 = 250/1000 = 1/4 = N2/N1
Answer
E1 = 4000 V, I1 = 250 A, I2 = 1000 A.
Where marks go

3 marks for the principle with a labelled diagram, 1 for the primary voltage, 1 for the two currents.

V(node 3) = 10 V

Q24 marksThevenin with a dependent source
For the circuit as shown in Figure 2, draw the Thevenin equivalent circuit across AB. (10 V source, 5 Ω carrying ix, a dependent current source 2ix, and 10 Ω across the terminals.)

Step 1 — why Rth cannot be found the usual way

The source is dependent, so it cannot be killed. Rth has to come from Vth and Isc, or from a test source. Killing it and writing 5 Ω ∥ 10 Ω is the wrong answer this question is designed to produce.

Step 2 — open-circuit voltage

With A–B open, the current arriving at the node from the 5 Ω and from the dependent source must all leave through the 10 Ω.

ix = (10 - Vm)/5 ix + 2ix = Vm/10 3(10 - Vm)/5 = Vm/10 6(10 - Vm) = Vm 60 = 7 Vm Vm = 60/7 = 8.571 V = V_th

Step 3 — short-circuit current

Shorting A to B pulls the node to 0 V, which makes ix easy to read off directly.

with Vm = 0: ix = 10/5 = 2 A the 10 ohm now carries nothing, so everything goes through the short: I_sc = ix + 2ix = 3 x 2 = 6 A

Step 4 — Rth

R_th = V_th/I_sc = (60/7)/6 = 10/7 = 1.429 ohm
Answer
Vth = 60/7 = 8.57 V, Rth = 10/7 = 1.43 Ω.
Where marks go

1 mark for recognising the dependent source cannot be killed, 1 for Vth, 1 for Isc, 1 for Rth.

Q33 marksRC charge and discharge
An RC charging-discharging circuit is shown in Figure 3 (R1 = 220 kΩ, C = 0.5 µF, R2 = 110 kΩ, E = 150 V). The switch has been in position 1 for a long time. (i) The switch is moved to position 2 — find the time constant and the voltage stored in the capacitor, and write the expression for the charging of the capacitor voltage. (ii) After the capacitor is fully charged the switch is moved back to position 1 — find the expression for the voltage across the capacitor.

Step 1 — read which loop each position makes

The pivot of the switch connects up through R1 and C to the right-hand rail. In position 1 the pivot goes to R2, so the loop is C–R1–R2 with no source in it. In position 2 the pivot goes to E, so the loop is C–R1–E and R2 is out.

position 1: discharge loop, R1 + R2, no source position 2: charge loop, R1 only, driven by E

Step 2 — part (i), charging

It sat in position 1 for a long time, so it had fully discharged and starts from zero.

tau_charge = R1 C = 220e3 x 0.5e-6 = 0.11 s final voltage = E = 150 V (no current at steady state, so no drop across R1) v(t) = 150 [1 - e^(-t/0.11)] V

Step 3 — part (ii), discharging

Now the capacitor starts fully charged at 150 V and has no source, so it decays to zero. The discharge path includes both resistors, which is why this time constant is larger.

tau_discharge = (R1 + R2) C = 330e3 x 0.5e-6 = 0.165 s v(t) = 150 e^(-t/0.165) V

Worth noticing

The two time constants differ because the charging path and the discharging path are not the same circuit. Assuming one time constant for both is the usual error here.

charging tau = 0.110 s (R1 alone) discharging tau = 0.165 s (R1 + R2) after 5 tau of charging: v = 148.99 V, i.e. 99.3% of 150 V
Answer
(i) τ = 0.11 s, final voltage 150 V, v(t) = 150(1 − e−t/0.11) V. (ii) v(t) = 150 e−t/0.165 V.
Where marks go

1 mark for each time constant, 1 for the two expressions. The marks are really for noticing that R2 is only in the discharge path.

Q43 marksHalf-rectified square wave
Calculate the rms value, average value, peak and form factor of a half-rectified square wave voltage shown in Figure 4. The time period of the waveform is 0.1 seconds and the peak value is 100 V.

A note on the figure

The time axis in the figure is labelled t(ms) while the text says the period is 0.1 seconds. The two do not match, but it makes no difference: rms, average, peak and form factor are all ratios over one period and none of them depends on the time scale at all.

Step 1 — rms

The wave is at 100 V for half the period and zero for the other half. Square, average, root.

mean of v^2 = (100^2 x T/2 + 0 x T/2)/T = 100^2/2 = 5000 V_rms = sqrt(5000) = 70.71 V = Vp/sqrt2

Step 2 — average

V_avg = (100 x T/2 + 0 x T/2)/T = 50 V = Vp/2

Step 3 — form factor and peak factor

form factor = V_rms/V_avg = 70.71/50 = 1.414 = sqrt2 peak factor = Vp/V_rms = 100/70.71 = 1.414 = sqrt2 verified by numerical integration over one full period: V_rms = 70.7107 V, V_avg = 50.0000 V

The comparison the examiner is after

For a full sine wave the form factor is 1.11 and the peak factor 1.414. For this half-rectified square wave both come out at 1.414. Saying so shows you understand that these factors describe the shape of the wave, not its size.

Answer
Peak = 100 V, Vrms = 70.71 V, Vavg = 50 V, form factor = 1.414.
Where marks go

1 mark each for rms, average and form factor. Averaging over only the conducting half instead of the full period gives 100 V and 70.71 V and loses two marks.

Q53 marksCast steel ring with a saw cut
A cast steel ring has a circular cross section 3 cm in diameter and a mean circumference of 80 cm. The ring is uniformly wound with a coil of 600 turns. The permeability of cast steel is 1000. (i) Estimate the current required to produce a flux of 0.5 mWb in the ring. (ii) If a saw cut 5 mm wide is made in the ring, find approximately the flux produced by the current found in (i).

Step 1 — part (i), the solid ring

A = pi d^2/4 = pi x 0.03^2/4 = 7.0686e-4 m^2 B = phi/A = 0.5e-3 / 7.0686e-4 = 0.7074 T H = B/(mu0 mu_r) = 0.7074/(4 pi 1e-7 x 1000) = 562.9 A/m mmf = H l = 562.9 x 0.80 = 450.3 AT I = mmf/N = 450.3/600 = 0.7505 A

Step 2 — part (ii), what the saw cut changes

The same current means the same mmf of 450.3 AT. But now that mmf has to push flux through 5 mm of air as well as 795 mm of iron, and air is a thousand times harder.

l_iron = 0.80 - 0.005 = 0.795 m l_gap = 0.005 m 450.3 = (B/mu0)[ 0.795/1000 + 0.005 ] = (B/mu0)[ 0.000795 + 0.005 ] = (B/mu0) x 0.005795 B = 450.3 x 4 pi 1e-7 / 0.005795 = 0.09765 T phi = B A = 0.09765 x 7.0686e-4 = 6.90e-5 Wb = 0.069 mWb

The result is the point of the question

A 5 mm cut in an 800 mm ring — less than one per cent of the path — drops the flux from 0.5 mWb to 0.069 mWb, a fall of about 86%. Look at the reluctance split and you can see why: the tiny gap now takes 0.005 of the 0.005795, which is 86% of the total.

check: H_iron l_iron + H_gap l_gap = 450.3 AT - correct reluctance share: iron 13.7%, air gap 86.3%
Answer
(i) I = 0.75 A. (ii) φ ≈ 0.069 mWb — about 14% of the original flux.
Where marks go

1.5 marks for part (i), 1.5 for part (ii). A sentence on why such a small gap matters so much is usually rewarded.

Q64 marksCoil on AC supply
A coil having a resistance of 5 Ω and inductance of 30 mH in series is connected across a 230 V, 50 Hz supply. Calculate (i) current in the circuit, (ii) power factor, (iii) active, reactive and apparent power, (iv) draw the phasor diagram.

Q74 marksBalanced star with two wattmeters
A balanced star-connected load is supplied from a symmetrical 3-phase, 440 V, 50 Hz supply system. The current in each phase is 20 A and lags behind its phase voltage by an angle of 40°. Calculate (i) phase voltage, (ii) load parameters, (iii) total power, (iv) reading of two wattmeters connected in the load circuit to measure total power.

Step 1 — phase voltage and impedance

Vph = VL/sqrt3 = 440/1.732 = 254.03 V Zph = Vph/Iph = 254.03/20 = 12.70 ohm

Step 2 — load parameters

R = Zph cos40 = 12.70 x 0.766 = 9.73 ohm XL = Zph sin40 = 12.70 x 0.643 = 8.16 ohm L = XL/(2 pi f) = 8.16/(2 pi x 50) = 0.026 H = 26 mH

Step 3 — total power

P = sqrt3 VL IL cos(phi) = 1.732 x 440 x 20 x cos40 = 11 676 W

Step 4 — the two wattmeter readings

The standard expressions. Note the two readings are very different even at a fairly good power factor.

W1 = VL IL cos(30 - phi) = 440 x 20 x cos(-10) = 8666 W W2 = VL IL cos(30 + phi) = 440 x 20 x cos(70) = 3010 W check: W1 + W2 = 11 676 W - equals the total power check: sqrt3 (W1 - W2)/(W1 + W2) = tan(phi) -> phi = 40.00 deg - recovers the given angle exactly
Answer
Vph = 254 V, R = 9.73 Ω, L = 26 mH, P = 11.68 kW, W1 = 8666 W, W2 = 3010 W.

1 mark for Vph, 1 for the load parameters, 1 for the total power, 1 for the two readings. The two checks in step 4 take one line each and prove the whole answer.

Q86 marksTransformer numerical and phasor diagram
(a) A 25 kVA transformer has 500 turns on the primary and 50 turns on the secondary winding. The primary is connected to a 3000 V, 50 Hz supply. Find (i) full load primary current, (ii) the induced voltage in the secondary winding, (iii) the maximum flux in the core. (b) Draw the phasor diagram of a practical transformer at no load.

Part (a) — the three quantities

(i) I1 = S/V1 = 25 000/3000 = 8.33 A (ii) E2 = V1 x N2/N1 = 3000 x 50/500 = 300 V (iii) phi_m = V1/(4.44 f N1) = 3000/(4.44 x 50 x 500) = 0.02703 Wb = 27.03 mWb

Cross-checks

E2 from the EMF equation: 4.44 x 50 x 50 x 0.02703 = 300 V - matches (ii) I2 = S/E2 = 25 000/300 = 83.3 A I1/I2 = 8.33/83.3 = 0.1 = N2/N1 - correct

Part (b) — the no-load phasor diagram

At no load the secondary carries no current, so the diagram is much simpler than the on-load one. Draw and label:

1. phi_m along the reference 2. E1 and E2 lagging phi_m by 90 degrees 3. I0 - the no-load current, leading phi_m by a small angle Imag = I0 sin(phi0), in phase with phi_m (magnetising) Iw = I0 cos(phi0), in phase with V1 (core loss) 4. V1 = -E1 (plus the very small I0 R1 and I0 X1 drops) phi0 is close to 90 degrees, so the no-load power factor is very poor - typically 0.1 to 0.2. Answer
I1 = 8.33 A, E2 = 300 V, φm = 27.03 mWb, plus the labelled no-load phasor diagram.
